Startup success can be engineered by following the right process. That means
the process can be learned, and if it can be learned, it can be taught.

Ries builds the argument around a contrast between myth and method:

- **The myth:** startup success is determined by talent, hard work, and having
  the right product.
  - The belief is false. It is a product of selection bias and after-the-fact
    rationalization, sustained by a "mythmaking industry."
  - It is also attractive. It explains failure through external factors - *we
    lacked the right qualities* - and lets people ignore the "boring" details of
    how the work is done.
  - Most startups, products, and ideas do not survive. Promising beginnings
    regularly end in failure, so "the right product plus hard work" is not
    sufficient for success.
    - [ET] One possible reason is precisely the widespread belief in this myth
      and the resulting concentration on the product.
- **The method:** "the boring stuff is the most important." Success does not
  come from the right genes or a lucky place and time; it comes from following
  the right process.
  - If success can be engineered through a process, the process can be learned.
    If it can be learned, it can also be taught.
  - One consequence is that entrepreneurship is a kind of management, despite
    the apparently opposite associations of the two words. See [A startup rejects predictable-domain management, not management itself](/en/claims/startup-management-uncertainty/).

- [ET] Not knowing the process for turning product ideas into a successful
  business practically guarantees failure.
  - Ries makes that claim only about his first startup: "we were doomed from day
    one, because we did not know the process." Extending it to every startup is
    an extrapolation from a single case.
