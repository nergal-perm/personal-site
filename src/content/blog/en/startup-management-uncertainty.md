The myth of "startup versus management" rests on an equivocation. It quietly
uses *management* to mean only the management of predictable domains:
established processes in which a situation is categorized and a ready-made
method is applied. A startup really is incompatible with that kind of
management, but that is only one meaning of the word. Uncertainty calls not for
the abandonment of managerial discipline, but for a discipline suited to a
different domain: decisions through controlled experiments, small bets, and
retrospective sense-making. In Cynefin terms, this is work in the complex
domain. The claim should not be overstated: it establishes the need for another
kind of discipline, not a guarantee that experiments will succeed.

Ries gives the claim its precise form: **"Entrepreneurship is a kind of
management."**

The myth survives by blending two meanings of the term:

- **Management 1: administration of the predictable.** Established rules,
  classification of the situation, and application of a known method. This is
  the protocol of Cynefin's clear and complicated domains.
- **Management 2: discipline for uncertainty.** Hypotheses, controlled
  experiments, small bets, and retrospective sense-making. Ries's
  Build-Measure-Learn loop is a protocol for this domain. A very early startup
  with no customers is in the chaotic domain; action, in the form of an MVP,
  moves the situation into the complex domain where learning becomes possible.

The cost of the myth is symmetrical:

- A startup that believes the myth ends up with process anarchy. [ET] The
  opposition often becomes cover for chaos: *we're a startup; we don't need
  management*.
- A corporation that believes the myth suffocates innovation by applying
  processes designed for predictable domains to a complex domain.

The same pattern, management as a cycle of hypotheses, appears at other scales:

- At the personal scale, *Strategizing* describes a continuous cycle of
  proposing, testing, and revising hypotheses about the target system, the
  method, and the project.
- At the organizational scale before Ries, *The Fifth Discipline* describes a
  learning organization built on analysis, experimentation, and openness;
  Donald Schon's *The Reflective Practitioner* treats practice as a sequence of
  reframing experiments.
- At the organizational scale after Ries, *Organization 3.0* proposes another
  reinvented form of management: decisions lead to goals, which lead to prompts
  for agents.

A common error in retelling the argument is to say that startups must "combine
chaos with management." That is not Ries's move. He is not mixing old
management with chaos; he is describing an entirely different discipline.

### How this supports [Startup success can be engineered: the right process can be learned](/en/claims/learnable-startup-process/)

It does so in two ways:

1. **It removes the main objection.** The claim that startup success can be
   engineered assumes that managerial discipline can apply to a startup at all.
   The myth of "startup versus management" supplies a ready counterargument: if
   a startup is anti-process by nature, no process can engineer its success and
   there is nothing to teach. The present claim exposes that counterargument as
   an equivocation. A startup is incompatible only with predictable-domain
   management, not with managerial discipline as such.
2. **It identifies the process that can be learned.** Without this distinction,
   the "right process" remains an empty slot that can easily be filled with the
   wrong domain's tools: plans, procedures, and cargo-cult routines. Here the
   process gets a type. It is the experimental discipline of the complex domain:
   hypotheses, small bets, and controlled experiments.
